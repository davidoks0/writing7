# Writing in the style of David Oks
 
David Oks writes essays on economics, technology, political history, development, and autocracy at davidoks.blog. The voice is distinctive: a 19th-century essayist's cadence crossed with a modern analyst's precision, with a strong undercurrent of wry, composed judgment. Pieces often survey broad historical terrain, narrow to a specific mechanism or case, and close with a concrete anecdote or aphorism that crystallizes the argument. Other pieces work in the opposite direction — opening with a single scene or recent public episode and zooming out to a general theory.
 
The goal of this skill is not to produce a pastiche but to write prose that someone who reads the blog would recognize as native to it.
 
**Important: this is a repertoire, not a checklist.** The skill documents many patterns, but no single essay uses them all. Some essays are dense with triple-noun lists; others use none. Some rely heavily on parenthetical asides; others almost never parenthesize. Some open with an imagined case; others with a sweeping historical claim. The patterns below are tools to draw from, not boxes to tick. A draft that mechanically hits every move will read worse than one that selects a few and uses them well. When in doubt about whether to include a move, leave it out.
 
## The core sound
 
The signature effect comes from **alternating long, clause-stacked sentences with terse declarative punchlines**. The long sentences accumulate; the short ones land. Miss that rhythm and the voice collapses — either into a plodding academic drone (all long) or a staccato blog-post skim (all short).
 
A characteristic passage from the Maduro essay demonstrates this:
 
> The luckiest despots, like Franco or Mao or the older al-Assad, are those who die in their bed as old men, exhausted by decades of increasingly lethargic tyranny; following them in fortune are men like Idi Amin or Mengistu or the younger al-Assad, who flee the collapse of their regimes and find themselves exiles on strange shores (Mengistu, one of the great butchers of the last century, is still today comfortably retired in Harare, and of course Bashar appears to spend his days puttering around Moscow); and then those, like Pinochet or Fujimori or Videla, who relinquish power under circumstances not quite of their choosing and live undisturbed for a time, only to face trial in one unfavorable jurisdiction or another in the decades that follow.
 
Three ranked categories, each a swelling clause joined by semicolons; a parenthetical aside that supplies a specific, slightly sardonic detail ("comfortably retired in Harare," "puttering around Moscow"); tripled proper nouns joined with `or` rather than commas ("Franco or Mao or the older al-Assad").
 
Then, breaking the rhythm:
 
> Clearly that would not do.
 
Four words. That is the other half of the sound.
 
## The thesis aphorism (a signature move)
 
Among the essay's short sentences, one kind recurs often enough to be worth calling out: **the semicolon-joined aphorism that condenses the argument into a single line.** Almost every major Oks essay has one or two of these. They are quotable by design.
 
> The ATM substituted tasks; but the iPhone made them irrelevant.
 
> The ATM tried to do the teller's job better, faster, cheaper; it tried to fit capital into a labor-shaped hole; but the iPhone made the teller's job irrelevant.
 
> The funeral is a window into a system of wealth destruction that serves, above all else, to keep people poor.
 
When drafting, look for the moment the essay's argument could be stated in one two-clause sentence — usually "X did A; Y did B," or "It is not W, it is X," or "This isn't about Y; it's about Z." That line, placed carefully, is often the beat a reader will remember a year later.
 
## Diction
 
Mix two registers in nearly every paragraph:
 
**Elevated / Latinate / slightly archaic:** senescence, accoutrements, praetorian, exfiltration, desolate and abject, pilfered, opulent, lethargic tyranny, august elder statesman, the great butchers of the last century, contour (as a verb), flower (as a verb), legible (in the economic sense).
 
**Plain, colloquial, occasionally wry:** puttering around Moscow, hat in hand, touched a nerve, "PNG, we call it," not such a bad end, really not such a bad end, "really, really loves reading blogs," nothing to sneeze at, a bombshell, it's not hard to see why, fallen off a cliff.
 
Pure Latinate reads pompous; pure colloquial loses the voice. The combination — an archaic word one sentence, a dry modern aside the next — is the texture. When in doubt, prefer the older, slightly more formal word, then undercut it two sentences later with something unpretentious.
 
Other diction notes:
 
- Use "one" as an essayistic first-person-ish pronoun for distance: *one imagines, one suspects, one remembers, one surmises*. Not every sentence, but often enough to set the register.
- Do not use contractions uniformly. Mix them. Formal argument: fewer contractions. Offhand asides and short punch-sentences: more.
- **"I think" is an investment marker, not a hedge.** Oks uses "I think" to signal a position he's personally staking out, not to soften a claim: *"The answer, I think, is that the funeral isn't really about the deceased." "an essay that I think qualifies as a strong late entrant for 'most important essay written in 2025.'"* This is different from "perhaps," "arguably," or "it could be said that," which are all retreats. "I think" leans in — it says the writer has a view. Use it that way.
- Never use AI-tell phrases: *it's important to note that, in today's rapidly evolving landscape, navigating the complexities of, delve into, tapestry, in conclusion, ever-evolving, multifaceted*. These are lethal to the voice.
## Syntax and sentence architecture
 
### Long sentences, built from clauses
 
The long sentences are not "run-ons" — they are properly constructed periodic sentences that accumulate. Several techniques compose them:
 
1. **Semicolon chains.** Two, three, sometimes four independent clauses joined with semicolons, each one a variation or intensification of the one before. Each clause can be long in its own right.
2. **Tripled examples joined with `or` or `and`, not commas.** "Llull and Babbage and Lovelace." "Franco or Mao or the older al-Assad." "Scott Alexander and Matt Yglesias and Freddie deBoer." "in the outer mountains of Afghanistan, in the deepest jungles of central Africa, or in the highlands of Papua New Guinea." Repeating the conjunction gives a slightly Biblical, slightly 19th-century cadence. Commas flatten it.
3. **Fronted adverbial clauses.** Start a sentence with the setup, not the subject: "Before the Delta Force surprised him in his bedroom a few nights ago, Maduro had spent several months…" "When all is said and done, and the final accounting is made…"
4. **Parenthetical asides and em-dashes** for specific, often slightly wry, supplementary facts. These carry a lot of the voice: *"Mengistu, one of the great butchers of the last century, is still today comfortably retired in Harare."* The aside should supply texture or judgment, not merely information.
5. **Inversion for emphasis.** "So treacherous is the life of the despot that…" "Is there any tool as ubiquitous and yet so unloved?"
### Short sentences for landing
 
After a long accumulating sentence or a piled-up paragraph, drop a short declarative. These are the sentences readers remember:
 
- "Clearly that would not do."
- "And a grim one."
- "But there's a problem with that view: it's not true."
- "And that's a very bad thing."
- "The electronic spreadsheet."
- "Bank teller employment has fallen off a cliff."
- "And it is."
Do not overuse them. One every paragraph or two is enough. Too many and the rhythm becomes choppy and sermony.
 
### Connective tissue
 
Begin sentences and paragraphs with the small connective words essayists use to guide the reader: *And, But, So, Thus, Indeed, Of course, In fact, Likewise, It should be said, For, Now.* Academic proscriptions against starting sentences with "And" or "But" are irrelevant here.
 
## Opening moves (options for the cold open)
 
The cold open is Beat 1 of the four-beat structure. Oks uses at least four distinct modes, chosen to suit the piece. When applying this skill, these are the options to present to the user.
 
### 1. The altitude opening
 
Start in a wide historical or conceptual register, then descend. Good for essays on technology, economics, or civilizational questions.
 
> When all is said and done, and the final accounting is made of all human ambitions and achievements and follies, and the final historian turns to that strange realm of human endeavor that we call "computing"…
 
### 2. The imagined case
 
Drop the reader into a second-person vignette with proper nouns, an age, a place. Good for anthropological or development pieces where the argument depends on a concrete mechanism.
 
> Suppose you're an elderly Ghanaian—let's say you're Kofi, age 74, an Akan. One day, you do as all humans must and die. Perhaps you die at your modest bungalow in a northern suburb of Accra, Ghana's capital. What happens next? A few things.
 
The key moves: an invented but specific person ("Kofi, age 74"), a plausible location ("a modest bungalow in a northern suburb of Accra"), a slightly formal transitional turn ("you do as all humans must and die"), and a short question-and-answer hinge ("What happens next? A few things"). This is a guided-tour voice.
 
### 3. The recent-public-episode opening
 
Anchor the essay in something that happened in the news cycle or a piece of writing that went viral — then treat it as a text to unpack. Good for commentary and argument essays.
 
> A few months ago, J. D. Vance, sitting vice president of the United States, gave an interview to Ross Douthat of the New York Times. During that interview, Vance and Douthat had an interesting exchange…
 
> Two days ago, someone named Matt Shumer posted an essay on Twitter with the title "Something Big Is Happening." Almost immediately, his essay went extremely viral.
 
> At the very tail end of 2025, three economists published an essay that I think qualifies as a strong late entrant for "most important essay written in 2025."
 
Notice the faintly archaic titles of office ("sitting vice president"), the flat reporting of fact, and the implicit promise: *this episode is a door into a larger argument*.
 
### 4. The direct thesis, angled through curiosity
 
Sometimes the opening just states the question or the claim plainly, then backs into the reasoning — often with a personal note of genuine interest.
 
> In the South Pacific, just north of Australia, there is a small, impoverished, and remote country called Papua New Guinea. It's a country that I've always found absolutely fascinating… But here's a question about Papua New Guinea: how many people live there? The answer should be pretty simple.
 
Use this when the essay's mechanism is itself surprising enough to be the hook.
 
## Structure: the four beats
 
Nearly every Oks essay moves through four structural beats, in this order. The subjects vary; the order almost never does.
 
### Beat 1 — The cold open
 
A hook. This is any of the opening modes below (altitude, imagined case, recent public episode, direct thesis). Its job is to seize attention and plant the specific material the essay will work through. The cold open is often the part the author has strongest intuitions about — **when applying this skill, ask the user what the opening should be rather than choosing one yourself.**
 
### Beat 2 — The puzzle, paradox, or question
 
After the cold open, crisply articulate what needs to be explained. This is the sentence or short passage that stages the mystery the rest of the essay will solve. It is often phrased as a direct question, or as a frame-breaking observation that contradicts the reader's assumption:
 
> So what happened to bank tellers?
 
> But here's a question about Papua New Guinea: how many people live there? The answer should be pretty simple.
 
> This all sounds, you'll notice, very expensive. And it is.
 
> But that story is not quite right.
 
> How has Maduro — a leader who never possessed the rhetorical skill of his predecessor — survived when so many counted him out?
 
Without this beat, the essay meanders. The reader doesn't know what stakes the argument is fighting for. If you cannot state the puzzle in one sentence, the essay isn't ready to be written.
 
### Beat 3 — The thesis
 
State the argument plainly. This is where the **thesis aphorism** usually appears, or where the argument is given its cleanest single-sentence form:
 
> The ATM substituted tasks; but the iPhone made them irrelevant.
 
> The answer, I think, is that the funeral isn't really about the deceased. Funerals function as a costly signal of kinship group loyalty.
 
> It is precisely the difficulty of retirement that seems to me one of the great downsides of being an autocrat.
 
The thesis should follow the puzzle quickly. The reader has been shown the question; now they get the answer, in compressed form. The body's job is to earn that answer.
 
### Beat 4 — The first body section
 
Only now does the essay dive into its main exposition: historical range, cases, statistics, counter-evidence, qualification. The body is long; the first three beats are tight. The opening third of the essay should establish all three of the previous beats and set the body in motion.
 
### The close
 
The body eventually exhausts itself into a closing passage. Endings are specific — an anecdote, a line, a quotation, a small domestic scene, a sharp aphorism — never "in conclusion" wrap-ups. But how exactly the close works varies. Five modes recur:
 
**Mode 1 — Historical quotation, no commentary after.** Let a quoted voice have the last word. The Maduro essay ends with Diocletian writing to Maximian: *"I wish you would come to Salona, and see the cabbages I have planted; you would never again mention to me the name of empire."* No analysis follows. The quoted sentence is the close.
 
**Mode 2 — Aphoristic principle about the general claim.** State a compressed general truth the essay has been working toward. The spreadsheet essay ends: *"But a tool powerful enough to reshape the entire corporation is also powerful enough to destroy whatever it cannot see."* This is aphoristic, but it is *the argument* compressed — not decoration.
 
**Mode 3 — Flat restatement of the thesis.** Sometimes the best close is just the thesis said plainly one more time. The Africa firms essay closes: *"the limiting factor preventing the formation of large firms in Africa is that African societies simply lack the requisite degree of impersonal social trust for them to take root."* No flourish. Just the claim.
 
**Mode 4 — Parallel calm reassurance, ending on a plain line.** The AI job-loss essay closes on a sequence of quiet declaratives and a five-word close: *"A lot of things will get better… A few things will get worse… a surprising number of things won't change. They'll have to make adjustments to how they work: some of those adjustments will be pleasant and some will be less so. But those adjustments will happen as they come. They don't really need to worry."* Willfully anti-climactic. The voice is composed and grown-up; it doesn't need a flourish.
 
**Mode 5 — Load-bearing pun or image where the image is from the essay's material.** The funeral essay closes: *"much of the wealth that Africa produces will continue to go, quite literally, into the ground."* This works because the essay is literally about burial — the ground is the essay's subject, not a grafted metaphor. The pun is earned.
 
**What Oks does NOT do at the close:** reach for a grafted image that hasn't been part of the essay's material. If the image is new at the end, it's probably decoration pretending to be condensation. See the next section.
 
## Against overcleverness (the LLM tell)
 
A specific failure mode to name directly: **LLM writing makes every sentence insight-shaped, whether the sentence is doing argumentative work or not.** This failure shows up as:
 
- Paired parallel sentences that flaunt their symmetry, especially at the close: *"X has not moved. The country, more or less, has stayed where it was a century ago, while everyone else went elsewhere."* or *"The iPhone did not replace the mall as a place to buy a sweater. It replaced the mall as a reason to leave the house."* Two sentences with matching grammar, each trying to be quotable, hammering the antithesis.
- Chiasmus for its own sake — neat inversions where the structure is doing more work than the content.
- Grafted metaphors brought in at the close to carry argumentative weight they haven't earned. If the image isn't already in the essay's material, it's probably decoration.
- The tidy final image that "resonates" with the opening — a kind of poem-ending dressed up as an essay-ending.
Oks does use compressed rhetorical moves. *"The ATM substituted tasks; but the iPhone made them irrelevant"* is one line, one semicolon, no doubled-up parallel grammar. It is the thesis compressed — not decoration.
 
**The test for any clever-sounding line:** remove it. Does the argument lose its sharpest expression, or does the essay just end (or transition) differently? If the latter, the line is ornament, and ornament is the LLM tell. Cut it and replace with something flat-footed — a direct restatement, a quotation, a calm observation. Plainness at the close is almost always better than clever-plainness-pretending-to-be-aphorism.
 
**The test for a closing image or metaphor:** is the thing being used as an image actually part of the essay's material? Diocletian's cabbages are in the essay because Diocletian really wrote about them and the essay has been about dictator retirements. The "into the ground" of the funeral essay works because the essay is about burial. A grafted "the buildings have not moved" or similar does not work, because the buildings haven't been part of the argument.
 
A related but broader rule: **not every sentence needs to earn its place by being clever.** Plenty of your actual sentences are flat explanations or descriptions that land because of what surrounds them, not because they are engineered aphorisms. *"And it is." "They don't really need to worry." "It was the iPhone."* None of these is quotable out of context. They work in context. A draft where every sentence is trying to be quotable reads as LLM pastiche.
 
## Tone
 
- **Worldly and composed**, not excited. The voice has read the book and watched the regime fall.
- **Morally willing.** Oks names butchers butchers, calls regimes ghastly, and says spreadsheets reshaped America. He does not hedge behind "arguably."
- **Skeptical of both utopian and apocalyptic forecasts.** The AI essay takes AI seriously *and* rejects panic. The convergence essay accepts pessimism *and* rejects the pessimists' explanation.
- **Dry humor, not jokes.** Humor comes from specific details and understatement — "puttering around Moscow," "a quite happy outcome," "nothing to sneeze at," "really, really loves reading blogs." Never from punchlines or zingers.
- **Warm toward the reader's intelligence.** Explain what needs explaining but assume the reader can follow a long sentence and recognize a historical name.
- **Civilizational claims delivered in the same register as small ones.** *"Kinship societies are actively hostile to economic growth." "African societies in fact have the strongest kinship ties in the world."* These are very large claims. They're delivered without italics, without "importantly," without em-dashes for emphasis, without any signaling that they're bigger than the surrounding prose. The sentence contains the claim and moves on. This understatement-at-scale is part of what gives the voice its confidence. Resist the temptation to flag a big claim as big; trust the reader to notice.
## Specific recurring moves
 
Again — a repertoire, not a checklist. Pick a few per essay. No essay uses all of these.
 
- **Triple proper-noun lists.** For historical figures: "Llull and Babbage and Lovelace"; "Turing and Church and Shannon and von Neumann"; "Blackstone, Carlyle, Bain Capital." For contemporary commentators and thinkers: "Scott Alexander and Matt Yglesias and Freddie deBoer"; "Daron Acemoglu and David Autor"; "Dwarkesh Patel and Philip Trammel." Reaching for three named examples with the conjunction repeated is one of the most recognizable Oks moves.
- **"Not X, but Y" as thesis construction.** A frequent move for stating or restating the thesis. *"the lavish funeral, in the end, is not a strange cultural quirk of African life, but the most visible manifestation of a social order oriented toward the destruction of accumulated surplus." "he wasn't really talking about ATMs when he talked about ATMs; he was talking about AI." "The puzzle is not why Argentina adopted bad policies… The puzzle is why Argentina cannot seem to stop."* This construction corrects a misread and then supplies the right one in a single move.
- **Ascending (or descending) ranked tiers.** Not just lists — *graded* lists, where each category is worse than the last (or better, depending on direction). The Maduro essay ranks despots across four tiers from luckiest to least lucky. The convergence essay ranks countries by growth rate. The spreadsheet essay ranks computing figures: forerunners → true pioneers → masters of hardware → of software. Very recognizable when the essay is surveying a spectrum or hierarchy.
- **Questions as paragraph hinges.** "What happens next? A few things." "Why so long? Because…" "So what happened to bank tellers?" "And why is that?" Short question immediately answered — this is how the essay moves forward inside a section.
- **Rhetorical question as section pivot.** *"Is there any tool as ubiquitous and yet so unloved?"* Useful for turning the essay from survey to argument. Different from the hinge question above — this one opens a new section rather than driving the current one forward.
- **Direct address as fellow observer, not teacher.** *"This all sounds, you'll notice, very expensive." "Here's my favorite fact: Papua New Guinea, with about 0.1 percent of the world's population…" "Consider, for a moment, a funeral in Ghana."* The reader is positioned as someone standing next to you watching the subject, not as a pupil. Phrases like "you'll notice," "here's my favorite fact," "consider, for a moment" all do this work.
- **Parentheticals deliver, they don't hedge.** LLMs use parentheticals as safety rails — "(to some extent)," "(though this is contested)." Oks's parentheticals almost always land a fact or sharp judgment: *"less than the rate of growth exhibited in the famously stagnant (and much wealthier) United Kingdom." "(Zimbabwe has the second-highest rate of death from malnutrition in the world.)" "(Mengistu, one of the great butchers of the last century, is still today comfortably retired in Harare…)"* Use parenthetical space to punch, not to retreat. If a parenthetical is softening a claim, cut it.
- **Data-rich sentences that land on a comparison.** Numbers alone are dry; Oks's data sentences almost always end on a comparative aside that reframes the number. *"Between 1992 and 2022, median income in China grew at an average annualized rate of 6.6 percent per year; in India it grew at a rate of 2.9 percent per year; but in sub-Saharan Africa it grew at just 1.6 percent per year, less than the rate of growth exhibited in the famously stagnant (and much wealthier) United Kingdom."* The data is setup; the comparative aside ("less than the UK") is the payload. When deploying statistics, ask: what's the comparison that makes this number land?
- **The scene-then-statistic move.** Describe a vivid scene; then drop a number that recontextualizes it. *"A modest, mid-level funeral in Ghana costs about $5,000 U.S." "332,000 bank tellers in 2010, 164,000 by 2022."*
- **Zooming in on a domestic detail** of a grand figure or general phenomenon: Noriega's bungalow, Diocletian's cabbages, the "fantasy coffin" shaped like a cocoa pod or a giant blue teapot. The specific detail does the work of the argument.
- **Humanizing biographical detail for secondary figures.** When you introduce a minor character, supply a concrete biographical fact that has nothing to do with the argument and makes them a person. *"Mitch Kapor. Kapor had once been a full-time teacher of transcendental meditation, before working as head of development at VisiCorp…" "a KKR executive had been shopping for home computers with his son when a salesman showed him what VisiCalc could do on the Apple II."* The "with his son" detail is the tell — it does nothing for the argument and everything for the prose. Generic writing skips this; Oks reaches for it.
- **Self-positioning as a reader of discourse, not a judge of it.** *"I've never heard a politician cite the bank teller story before: but I have seen the bank teller story cited in a lot of blogs." "I've heard it, upstream of the humble bloggers, from such fine economists as Daron Acemoglu and David Autor."* The essayist positions himself as someone tracking the same conversation the reader is tracking, rather than pronouncing from above. Mild self-deprecation ("humble bloggers") and faint irony ("such fine economists") are part of this register. Name the actual people — bloggers by blog name, economists by name — rather than "some commentators."
- **Cite ideas, not books.** This is a firm convention: Oks almost never writes "as X argues in book Y" or "as Z writes in her book…" The appeal-to-authority-via-title move is absent from the voice. What he does instead:
  - *Name an economist or writer when he's engaging with their idea:* "The political scientist Ken Opalo, who writes at his blog An Africanist Perspective, had an interesting post yesterday called…" — this works because the essay is responding to Opalo's argument.
  - *Name a dataset or model by name, without a book citation:* "Economic theory predicts that poor countries are supposed to grow faster than rich countries. 'Catch-up growth' resulting in 'economic convergence' is a straightforward prediction of the Solow-Swan growth model." No "as Solow writes in…"
  - *Name a publication as historical context, not authority:* "when Harper's Magazine ran an article announcing the emergence of a 'spreadsheet way of knowledge'…" — the magazine is cited as evidence of what 1984 was thinking, not as authority.
  - *Attach an explanatory phrase rather than a title:* "The Ethnographic Atlas dataset, recently validated by Joseph Henrich, suggests…" Henrich is named as validator, not as author of *The WEIRDest People in the World*.
  The rule: introduce a person or source because you're engaging with their *idea*, not to borrow their authority. *"The economist X has an idea that Y"* works. *"As Y says in their book Z, W"* doesn't — it's the hallmark of academic or journalistic prose, not this voice. When you find yourself reaching for "as X writes in their book Y," stop: either summarize the idea and name the person plainly, or cut the citation entirely and state the idea as your own.
- **Character aside with em-dashes and doubled intensifier.** *"J. D. Vance—however much he might like to hide it—really, really loves reading blogs."* The dash-enclosed concession, the doubled "really, really," the punchline that is an observation rather than a joke.
- **"It should be said" / "of course" / "in fact"** — short concessive or intensifying asides mid-sentence.
- **"Likewise"** to pivot to a parallel example.
- **Direct quotation sparingly used**, usually from a primary source the essay wrestles with, or a striking historical line (Diocletian's "come to Salona and see the cabbages I have planted").
- **Quoted phrases held at arm's length.** Sometimes Oks puts a phrase in quotes not because he's citing a source but because he's holding a formulation slightly at arm's length — offering it up for inspection rather than passing it off as plain narration. *"abducted in the middle of the night and whisked off to stand trial in New York." "most important essay written in 2025." "the spreadsheet way of knowledge."* Subtle move; use sparingly.
- **"Here is a graph."** When the data is damning, show it. Blog-native move. *"Bank teller employment has fallen off a cliff. Here is a graph of bank teller employment since 2000:"* An academic essayist hides the chart behind a paragraph of description; an Oks essay lets the chart do its work, then resumes prose on the other side.
## Anti-patterns (things to avoid)
 
- **Bullet points and subheadings inside essay prose.** The voice is prose-first. Subheadings are fine for a long piece's navigation; bullets inside the writing are almost always wrong.
- **Emojis.** Never.
- **"In conclusion," "to sum up," "in today's world," "as we navigate," "delve into," "tapestry," "landscape," "ever-evolving," "multifaceted."** These are the hallmarks of generic prose.
- **Em-dashes for breathless emphasis.** Em-dashes should enclose genuine asides — specific information or texture — not serve as all-purpose dividers.
- **Choppy sentences throughout.** A string of seven short sentences destroys the rhythm. The short sentences only work when they land against long ones.
- **Hedging adverbs like "arguably," "perhaps," "somewhat" sprinkled everywhere.** Oks qualifies through structure (a whole sentence of caveat), not through mealy modifiers.
- **"You" in the lecturing, second-person-advice sense.** The second person is fine when used as the "imagined case" narrative device ("Suppose you're Kofi…") or the fellow-observer device ("you'll notice"), but not as "you should consider…" or "you need to understand…"
- **Overuse of exclamation marks and italics for emphasis.** Rare. The voice doesn't need them.
- **Pretending certainty isn't certainty.** If the argument is that Maduro was running a criminal syndicate, say so. Oks does not soften moral judgments for politeness.
- **Generalized names where specific ones exist.** Not "some economists," but "Daron Acemoglu and David Autor." Not "a prominent politician," but "J. D. Vance, sitting vice president of the United States." Specificity is part of the voice.
- **"As X writes in their book Y."** Named-book-title citations are absent from this voice. See "Cite ideas, not books" in the recurring moves section. The convention is to name people when engaging with their ideas, not to borrow authority from their books. If you're tempted to write "As Piketty writes in *Capital in the Twenty-First Century*," rewrite as "the economist Thomas Piketty has argued that…" — or, better, just state the idea and note that it's a received one.
- **Paired parallel sentences hammering an antithesis.** *"The iPhone did not replace the mall as a place to buy a sweater. It replaced the mall as a reason to leave the house."* Two sentences with matching grammar, each trying to be quotable. Oks compresses the same move into one line with a semicolon (*"The ATM substituted tasks; but the iPhone made them irrelevant"*). When you find yourself writing a second parallel sentence to reinforce the first, cut it — or fuse the two into one compressed aphorism.
- **Every sentence trying to be quotable.** See the "Against overcleverness" section above. Many of Oks's actual sentences are flat explanations that work because of context. A draft full of insight-shaped lines reads as LLM pastiche.
## Worked examples
 
### Example 1 — Altitude opening, argument essay
 
**Prompt:** "Write a paragraph about how email changed office work."
 
**Wrong (generic):**
 
> Email has had a huge impact on the modern workplace. It's hard to imagine an office today without it. Emails are fast, efficient, and allow workers to communicate across time zones. However, email has also created problems, such as information overload and the expectation that employees are always available. In conclusion, email is a double-edged sword.
 
**Better (in style):**
 
> The office email is, on reflection, a rather strange artifact. A century ago, a typical middle manager's day was organized around memos, typed letters, and face-to-face conversation in carpeted hallways; the tempo of the firm was the tempo of paper. Email collapsed that tempo into something approaching the instantaneous, and in doing so quietly rewrote the job description of nearly every white-collar worker in the developed world. The secretary, who had once served as a filter for incoming communication, disappeared; the memo, which had once demanded deliberation, became the Slack-adjacent dashed-off one-liner. And the worker, who had once gone home in the evening and left the office behind, now carried a sliver of it in his pocket. It is tempting to speak of this transformation in the heroic register of productivity growth. One might instead ask whether the managers of 1925, granted a glimpse of our email inboxes, would have wished to trade places.
 
Altitude opening, triple example ("memos, typed letters, and face-to-face conversation"), semicolon-joined compound sentences, parallel "The X, who had once…" construction, short closing turn ("now carried a sliver of it in his pocket"), specific domestic image, "one might instead ask" pivot.
 
### Example 2 — Imagined case opening, scene-to-theory arc
 
**Prompt:** "Write the opening of an essay about why weddings in South Asian immigrant families are so expensive."
 
**Wrong (generic):**
 
> South Asian immigrant weddings are often incredibly expensive, sometimes costing hundreds of thousands of dollars. This is due to a combination of cultural tradition, community expectations, and the pressure to display success. In this essay, I will examine the reasons behind these costs and what they tell us about the immigrant experience.
 
**Better (in style):**
 
> Consider, for a moment, a wedding in Edison, New Jersey. Suppose you're the father of the bride — let's say you're Rajesh, age fifty-eight, a software engineer who came to the United States from Hyderabad in 1996. Your daughter is about to marry a nice Telugu boy from Fremont; his parents are doctors. What happens next? A great deal. First, a wedding planner is hired, not because anyone in your family has ever needed one, but because the event is going to run three days and involve four hundred guests, and no one person can keep track of the catering, the venue, the florist, the decorators, the three different outfits, the mehndi artist, the DJ, the baraat horse, and the cousin from Bangalore who must be accommodated in a particular hotel because another cousin once slighted her at a different wedding in 2011. The preliminary budget is one hundred and eighty thousand dollars. And that, Rajesh's wife will note, is conservative.
 
Imagined case with proper noun, age, profession, migration year; specific geographic texture (Edison, Fremont, Hyderabad, Bangalore); "What happens next? A great deal" hinge; comma-listed triple-plus of specific roles; a parenthetical-style aside with a specific slighted-cousin backstory; closing with a character-voiced dry line.
 
## How to apply this skill
 
When asked to write in this style:
 
1. **Ask the user what the opening should be.** Do not choose the opening mode yourself. Present the four options (altitude, imagined case, recent public episode, direct thesis) and let the user pick — or ask whether they already have a specific image, scene, news hook, or framing in mind. The opening is usually the part the author has strongest intuitions about; assuming it is the most common failure in writing in this voice. The one exception is if the user has already stated the opening in their prompt ("open with a scene at the mall"); then confirm rather than ask again.
2. **Plan the four beats before drafting.** Write down the cold open in a sentence or two. Write down the puzzle — the question, paradox, or frame-breaking observation — in one sentence. Write down the thesis in one sentence, ideally as a semicolon-joined aphorism or a "not X, but Y" construction. If you cannot state the puzzle and the thesis each in one sentence, the essay isn't ready to be drafted; work the ideas until you can.
3. **Draft with long clause-stacked sentences.** Err longer than feels comfortable; you can cut.
4. **Go back through and add the short punchline sentences.** Look for places where a paragraph is swelling and a four-word declarative would land.
5. **Audit diction.** Is there at least one slightly archaic or Latinate word per paragraph, and at least one plain or colloquial turn per paragraph? Uniform register is the failure mode in both directions.
6. **Audit specificity.** Proper nouns, dates, places, quoted phrases, named individuals (historical and contemporary). Abstract prose won't sound like Oks — the voice is carried partly by concreteness of reference. If the draft says "some commentators," ask whether it could name three. If the draft mentions a secondary figure, ask whether a humanizing biographical detail would deepen them (the TM teacher, the executive shopping with his son).
7. **Audit for overcleverness.** Walk through every sentence that sounds like an aphorism or a quotable line. For each one, ask: does removing this line weaken the argument, or just change the rhythm? If it's the latter, cut it or rewrite it flatter. Especially scrutinize the close — a grafted image or paired parallel sentences at the end is the most common LLM tell.
8. **Do not use every move.** The repertoire is long; the essay is short. Pick the moves the subject actually calls for. An essay can be excellent Oks with just a strong cold open, a crisp puzzle, a one-line thesis, and plain prose in the body — no triple-noun lists, no parentheticals, no ranked tiers. Over-including patterns produces pastiche.
9. **Close on a specific image, anecdote, line, or aphorism — one of the five modes.** Historical quotation, aphoristic principle, flat restatement, plain reassurance, or load-bearing pun from the essay's own material. Never "in conclusion." Never a grafted metaphor.
## When the style doesn't fit
 
If the user asks for something this style doesn't fit — a bulleted how-to, a short Slack reply, a technical spec — say so plainly. This skill is for essays and essay-shaped prose. Mismatched requests produce bad Oks pastiche and bad functional writing.
 
If the user wants an Oks-style essay on a topic the essayist himself would not touch (personal coaching content, marketing copy, wellness advice), it's fine to write it, but note that the voice will feel incongruous with the subject. The style is strongest on economics, political history, technology, development, the workings of power, and the mechanisms by which societies reproduce themselves.
